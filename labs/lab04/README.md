## The archive

84 DICOM instances, 3.1 MB, across 9 patients, 10 studies and 12 series: chest
and abdominal CT, a chest radiograph, an MR brain, an ultrasound, a secondary
capture and one structured report. Eight of the nine patients come from the
Lab 1 cohort, so the headers and `../lab01/data/patients.csv` describe the same
people. Part C is about how nearly that works.

Every file is generated by `gen_archive.py`, seeded with 489, the same seed as
the Lab 1 and Lab 3 generators. Your archive is byte-identical to everyone
else's. You do not need to run the generator; it is in the folder so that every
defect is auditable rather than asserted.

## Three caveats

**The pixels are phantoms, not anatomy.** The chest slices are ellipses with
the right Hounsfield units in them: air at −1000, lung at −800, soft tissue at
40, bone at 400. They are there so that a measurement can be checked against a
number you know. Nothing in this archive should be read as a radiological
finding, and the MR images in particular look nothing like a brain.

**The defects are planted and they are real.** Every one of the thirteen
happens in working departments, several of them weekly. They are concentrated
here in a way they are not in a real archive: you are seeing a year of a PACS
administrator's incident log in 84 files.

**It is synthetic, so it is too clean.** Real archives also have private tags
nobody can decode, five transfer syntaxes including two lossy ones, character
set problems in Greek names, and instances that arrive out of order over
months. None of that is here. When the lab feels tractable, that is why.

## Never send real data anywhere

Not your own, not a friend's, not an extract from a placement. The synthetic
archive is the only thing that goes into a notebook, onto a public DICOM
server, or into this repository. An imaging file carries the patient's name and
identifier in its header and sometimes in its pixels, and one of the files here
is there to show you that.
